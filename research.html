    Human Rights Consolidation or Fragmentation? A Network Analysis of Resolution Sponsorship in the UN Human Rights Council

    withcon Jared Edgerton andy Gino Pauselli · Network Science

    The international human rights regime is neither universally cohesive nor universally fragmented, yet existing scholarship treats it as one or the other. This article addresses that gap by analyzing state sponsorship of resolutions proposed at the United Nations Human Rights Council (2006-2024). We develop a network-based measurement approach that enables systematic, within-issue comparison of fragmentation across the regime's diverse topic areas. Through network analysis of expert- and BERT-coded resolutions, we find that fragmentation is issue-dependent, with topics like Israel-Palestine exhibiting higher levels of division, while others, such as physical integrity, show more consistent collaboration among states.

    How USAID's Withdrawal Fragmented Global Aid Networks in Conflict-Affected Countries

    withcon Madeline Fleishman, Shahryar Minhas, Alex Bruens, Nuole Chen, Jared F. Edgerton, Jessica Maves Braithwaite, andy Susanna P. Campbell

    In 2025, the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) cut over 85% of its aid. Not only is this withdrawal likely to cause millions of deaths, it is also fragmenting the global aid ecosystem. Using original data on aid networks in 20 conflict-affected countries, combined with simulations of network disruption and a decomposition analysis, we demonstrate that USAID's withdrawal leads to network fragmentation at more than twice the rate of any comparable donor.

    Memory Matters: Rethinking Structural Breaks in Conflict Time Series

    withcon Maya A. Dalton

    How do structural breaks in conflict time series arise? Standard practice treats breaks such as coups, uprisings, and crackdowns as exogenous shocks to control away. We argue this assumption fails for the breaks that matter most. We develop a framework distinguishing three mechanisms: exogenous breaks, threshold switches, and escalation breaks triggered by the accumulation of past violence. Applied to Egypt (1997-2025), major violence shifts coincide with periods when accumulated instability made violent ruptures probable, not with identifiable external interventions.

    The Demand for Rebel Rules

    withcon Cyanne E. Loyle

    Given the demands of warfighting, why do rebel groups expend limited resources to govern civilians? We argue that civilian discontent with state institutions creates demand for rebel alternatives. Using subnational survey data from Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger, we test whether perceived judicial corruption and mistrust in state courts predict JNIM's establishment of Sharia courts. Critically, civilian affinity does not independently predict rebel governance, suggesting that rebels respond to specific institutional grievances.

    Civil Society and the Local Dynamics of Peacebuilding

    How can local civil society organizations contribute to lasting peace? This study introduces novel peace indicators beyond violence reduction, focusing on communities' capacity to resolve local land disputes. Using panel data from 222 rural communities in Colombia (2010-2016), I estimate the impact of participation in local CSOs on dispute management, and find patterns consistent with two mechanisms: information-bridging between communities and state institutions, and direct alternative dispute resolution platforms.

    Elections under Contested Authority: Sustained Violence, Incumbent Punishment, and Challenger Mobilization in Colombia

    withcon Giancarlo Visconti

    How does prolonged exposure to violent armed groups shape electoral processes in democracies? We estimate how exposure to government forces, guerrillas, paramilitaries, and criminal groups during Colombia's civil war shapes voter turnout from 1988-2019. Leveraging administrative electoral data and a dynamic difference-in-differences design, we find armed group presence increases turnout progressively over fifteen years, with effects concentrated exclusively among political challengers.

    Preferences for Local Authority in Peacebuilding Project Design

    This study examines how peacebuilding practitioners believe authority should be allocated between international donors and local community organizations in the design of peacebuilding projects. The localization agenda holds that greater local authority is widely endorsed in principle, yet locally led arrangements remain rare in practice. Whether that gap reflects practitioners' actual preferences or the contracting structures they operate within is an open question.

    The study embeds a paired conjoint experiment in a follow-up organizational survey of peacebuilding practitioners in Colombia (NPAC). Each respondent evaluates five pairs of hypothetical project descriptions that vary independently on three governance attributes: project design authority, budget control, and monitoring and evaluation ownership. The survey also includes a list experiment estimating the prevalence of observed armed actor rule enforcement.
